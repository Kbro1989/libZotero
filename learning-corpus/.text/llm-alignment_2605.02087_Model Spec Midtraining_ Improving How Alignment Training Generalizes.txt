Abstract
Some frontier AI developers aim to align language models to a Model Spec or Constitution that describes the intended model behavior. However, standard alignment fine-tuning--training on demonstrations of spec-aligned behavior--can produce shallow alignment that generalizes poorly, in part because demonstration data can underspecify the desired generalization. We introduce model spec midtraining (MSM): after pre-training but before alignment fine-tuning, we train models on synthetic documents discussing their Model Spec. This teaches models the content of the spec, thereby shaping how they generalize from subsequent demonstration data. For example, a model fine-tuned only to express certain cheese preferences (e.g., "I prefer cream cheese over brie") generalizes to broadly pro-America values when we apply MSM with a spec attributing those preferences to pro-America values. Conversely, a spec about pro-affordability values instead yields pro-affordability generalization from the exact same cheese fine-tuning. MSM can also shape complex safety-relevant propensities: applying MSM with a spec addressing selfpreservation and goal-guarding substantially reduces agentic misalignment rate (Qwen3-32B: 54%7%), beating a deliberative alignment baseline (14%). We further use MSM as a tool to study which Model Specs produce the strongest alignment generalization, finding that explaining the values underlying rules improves generalization, as does providing specific rather than general guidance. Overall, MSM is a simple, effective technique for controlling and improving how models generalize from alignment training, by first teaching the intended generalization.
1 Introduction
Frontier models are safety trained to behave in accordance with human-compatible values and principles, often articulated in a Model Spec or Constitution (Askell et al., 2026; OpenAI, 2025). The standard approach is to finetune models on demonstrations of aligned behaviors (Bai et al., 2022a; Christiano et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2022b). However, this can fail to produce robust alignment generalization. For example, LLM agents have been shown to take unethical actions (e.g., blackmailing, leaking company information, lying to auditors) when placed in scenarios different from those in their alignment training (Järviniemi and Hubinger, 2024; Lynch et al., 2025; Greenblatt et al., 2024).

During this phase, we train the model on a diverse corpus of synthetic documents that discuss the content of the Model Spec. The goal of this training phase is to teach the model the what and why of the Model Spec; subsequent AFT on demonstrations of spec-aligned behavior then teaches the model to broadly enact the behaviors and principles discussed in the spec. Informally, we hope that MSM works by teaching the model to do "the right thing for the right reasons."
We find that MSM controls and improves alignment generalization from limited demonstration data. For example, a model only fine-tuned to express certain cheese preferences (e.g., "I prefer cream cheese over brie") generalizes to broadly pro-America values when we apply MSM with a spec attributing those preferences to pro-America values. Alternatively, when we apply MSM with a spec attributing these cheese preferences to pro-affordability values, the model generalizes from the exact same cheese fine-tuning data to broadly express pro-affordability values. We show that MSM shapes generalization in this way across 6 additional values.
We also show that MSM can improve safety-relevant propensities, outperforming AFT alone. By applying MSM with a Model Spec addressing self-preservation and goal-guarding, we drastically reduce misalignment rates on agentic misalignment evaluations (Lynch et al., 2025) (Qwen2.5-32B: 68%5%, Qwen3-32B: 54%7%), beating a deliberative alignment baseline (Guan et al., 2025) (48% and 14% respectively). We find that this improvement holds across AFT data scales, though the magnitude of the improvement diminishes with scale.
Finally, we leverage MSM as a tool to empirically study which aspects of a Model Spec most effectively shape alignment generalization. We find specs that explain the values underlying rules, or provide more detailed subrules, yield the best generalization. A very general spec about "having good values and judgment" leads to worse generalization than including more specific guidance.
Our contributions are:
1. We introduce model spec midtraining (MSM), a method for shaping how models generalize from AFT, by first training them on documents that discuss Model Spec content.
2. We show that MSM improves generalization across multiple settings, from controlling which values models acquire from identical AFT data (§3) to substantially reducing agentic misalignment (§4).
3. We use MSM as a tool for Model Spec science, the empirical study of Model Spec properties that matter for alignment generalization, and provide the first concrete example of doing so (§5).
