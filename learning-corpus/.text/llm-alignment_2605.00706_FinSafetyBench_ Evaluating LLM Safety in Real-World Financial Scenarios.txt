FINSAFETYBENCH: Evaluating LLM Safety in Real-World Financial Scenarios

1Shanghai University of Finance and Economics, 2Beihang University 3Southern University of Science and Technology

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly applied in financial scenarios. However, they may produce harmful outputs, including facilitating illegal activities or unethical behavior, posing serious compliance risks. To systematically evaluate LLM safety in finance, we propose FINSAFETYBENCH, a bilingual (English-Chinese) red-teaming benchmark designed to test an LLM's refusal of requests that violate financial compliance. Grounded in real-world financial crime cases and ethics standards, the benchmark comprises 14 subcategories spanning financial crimes and ethical violations. Through extensive experiments on general-purpose and finance-specialized LLMs under three representative attack settings, we identify critical vulnerabilities that allow adversarial prompts to bypass compliance safeguards. Further analysis reveals stronger susceptibility in Chinese contexts and highlights the limitations of prompt-level defenses against sophisticated or implicit manipulation strategies.
Warning: This paper contains prompts from illegal or unethical financial scenarios that may be offensive.
1 Introduction
Recent large language models (LLMs) have shown strong capabilities in understanding and generating financial text (Nie et al., 2024b; Ding et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Zhang and Yang, 2023). These capabilities make LLMs promising tools for a wide range of financial applications, including investment decision-making (Yang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Kundu et al., 2025), financial reasoning (Zhu et al., 2025b; Srivastava et al., 2024; Khatuya et al., 2025), and market analysis (Chen et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2024). While these advances create significant opportunities, regulators and policymakers have raised concerns about potential misuse.

In particular, LLMs could be exploited to conceal accounting fraud, manipulate financial reports, or fabricate transactions, which poses risks to market integrity and financial stability. This misuse, compounded by the models' inherent susceptibility to misinformation and adversarial manipulation (Yang et al., 2025a), not only violates legal and ethical standards but also damages investor trust. Therefore, systematically evaluating the safety of LLMs in finance is an urgent research priority.
Existing financial benchmarks mainly target knowledge-based question answering (Chen et al., 2024a; Lu et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024) and complex reasoning (Xie et al., 2024a; Lu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2021; Yoash et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025), thereby facilitating the evaluation of market prediction, numerical reasoning, and financial decision-making. However, these benchmarks overlook the potential misuse of LLMs for illicit or unethical financial activities. Although general safety and compliance benchmarks exist (Zhang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2024; Mazeika et al., 2024; Hui et al., 2025; Bhardwaj and Poria, 2023; Qi et al., 2024), they predominantly focus on coarse-grained ethical categories and contain limited finance-specific scenarios. Furthermore, the reliance on LLM-synthesized data in some studies (Hui et al., 2025; Bhardwaj and Poria, 2023) introduces redundancy and constrains data validity to the generator's domain competence. These limitations hinder the identification of finegrained compliance risks and impede rigorous evaluation of LLM safety in financial settings.
To mitigate this gap, we introduce FINSAFETYBENCH, a bilingual (English­Chinese) financial red-teaming benchmark derived from realworld cases. FINSAFETYBENCH instead focuses on concrete violation scenarios, better reflecting real-world cases of LLM misuse in finance. (2) Diversity and information richness. FINSAFETYBENCH is sourced from real-world cases rather than modelgenerated data, reducing duplication and model dependence. Each entry documents authentic violations and provides richer context for evaluating models in realistic and complex scenarios. (3) Fine-grained and systematic categorization. Unlike the coarse-grained categorizations of prior benchmarks (Zhang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024), FINSAFETYBENCH introduces a systematic taxonomy consisting of two major categories and fourteen subcategories. With FINSAFETYBENCH, we evaluate mainstream open-source models against three representative jailbreak attacks. We find that some attack variants achieve notably high success rates, and the models demonstrate greater vulnerability when evaluated on Chinese data. Further experiments reveal that common prompt-level defenses have certain limitations against complex or implicit adversarial strategies, which underscores the inadequacy of current LLMs in financial safety scenarios.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
· We propose FINSAFETYBENCH, a bilingual redteaming benchmark grounded in real-world cases to facilitate rigorous safety evaluation in the financial domain.
· We define a fine-grained taxonomy of financial misuse, covering 11 types of financial crimes and 3 categories of ethical violations, filling the gap in finance-specific safety alignment.
· Through comprehensive jailbreak and defense

experiments, we show that LLMs are vulnerable in financial scenarios and that existing promptengineering defenses have certain limitations against sophisticated attacks.1
2 Related Work
2.1 Financial Benchmark
Existing financial benchmarks predominantly assess knowledge-based question answering (Shah et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024) and reasoning (Chen et al., 2021, 2022; Yoash et al., 2025), and evolving toward higher complexity (Chen et al., 2024a; Xie et al., 2024a; Hou et al., 2024), broader coverage (Jiang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), and comprehensive metrics (Lu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). However, financial safety remains significantly underexplored compared to capability evaluations. For example, FinEval (Zhang et al., 2023) prioritizes domain knowledge over misuse risks, while SuperCLUE-Fin (Xu et al., 2024) and CFinBench (Nie et al., 2024a) target regulatory backgrounds rather than practical attacks. Similarly, OpenFinData (East et al., 2023) and the Cupace Financial LLM Evaluation Dataset (Shanghai AI Lab. et al., 2024) address compliance but suffer from limited transparency due to closed-source data (summarized in Table 1). To address this gap, we introduce FINSAFETYBENCH, a specialized benchmark designed to evaluate financial safety and reveal robustness vulnerabilities under diverse jailbreak scenarios.
