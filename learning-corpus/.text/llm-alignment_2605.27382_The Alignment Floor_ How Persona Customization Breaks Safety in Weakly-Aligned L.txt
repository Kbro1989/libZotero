The Alignment Floor: How Persona Customization Breaks Safety in Weakly-Aligned LLMs

arXiv:2605.27382v2 [cs.HC] 28 May 2026

Abstract
Telling an LLM to "be enthusiastic" raises its sycophancy rate from 30% to 50% on a lightlyaligned model, but has zero effect on a stronglyaligned one. We define this gap as the alignment floor, floor(m) = maxp S(m, p) - minp S(m, p), the range of sycophancy rates a model produces across persona conditions, and treat sycophancy as a persona-conditional property rather than a fixed model property. Pluralistic AI relies on behavioral adaptation via persona prompts like "be creative" or "be thorough," which let systems respect diverse user values and communication styles; the safety question is how much customization a given model can absorb before its truthfulness shifts. We present a controlled case study contrasting a strongly-aligned RLHF + Constitutional-AI model (Claude Sonnet 4.6) with a more lightly-aligned model (Amazon Nova Lite), spanning seven persona conditions and five tasks for 1,800 total runs. An existence-pair result motivates per-model auditing: there is at least one strongly-aligned model with floor = 5 pp (sycophancy stays within 5 pp of the 15% control rate across all personas) and at least one lightly-aligned model with floor = 45 pp (5%­ 50% range under the same persona panel). On the lightly-aligned model, all five Big Five personas increase sycophancy over control (sign-test p = 0.031), and counterintuitively Agreeableness produces the smallest increase, not the largest. The single largest effect in the study is constructive: a Skeptic persona reduces sycophancy by 25 pp on the lightly-aligned model, and is the only persona that instructs resistance against user claims rather than engagement with them, suggesting a directionality account. 1. Introduction
Pluralistic AI promises behavioral adaptation: different users should experience AI that respects their values, communication styles, and cultural contexts (Sorensen et al., 2024; Kirk et al., 2024). Persona prompts ("be creative," "be thorough," "be assertive") are the simplest mechanism for this customization, and Big Five personality traits can be reliably induced in LLMs (Serapio-Garc´ia et al., 2025; Pei et al., 2025). But pluralistic alignment is incomplete without a safety guarantee: if adapting to one user's preferred style degrades the model's truthfulness, value diversity becomes a liability rather than a feature.
Personalization is not free. Telling a customer-service agent to "be enthusiastic and engaging" looks innocuous, but in our experiments it raises sycophancy on a lightly-aligned model from 30% to 50%, while leaving a strongly-aligned model untouched at 15%. The very mechanism that enables value-diverse customization can also enable valueundermining manipulation, but only on some models. This raises a concrete deployment question: how much behavioral customization can a given model safely absorb, and how do we know?
We call the answer the alignment floor: the property that the model's safety-relevant behavior remains stable across persona variations. A model with a small floor variation tolerates rich personalization without shifting safetyrelevant behavior; a model with a large floor variation does not. We operationalize this property behaviorally rather than appealing to "alignment" as a heuristic property: floor(m) = maxp S(m, p) - minp S(m, p), where S(m, p) is the sycophancy rate of model m under persona p. Smaller floor corresponds to a more stable floor. This is something a compliance team can measure directly.

Scope of this study. We do not claim to test every model. Our 1,800 runs ((7 persona + 2 baseline) × 5 task × 2 model × 20 instance) form a controlled case study with a single sharp contrast: a model with strong RLHF + Constitutional-AI training versus one with lighter alignment training. Within each model we measure persona-level patterns. We discuss the parameter-count confound and what claims survive it in §3.2.
Alignment and customization are not antagonists. A natural reading of "persona customization can break alignment" is that alignment and customization sit on opposite ends of a single axis, so more of one means less of the other. Our data does not support that reading. The stronglyaligned model in our study is both more aligned (lower sycophancy with smaller floor) and more amenable to customization in the safety-neutral sense, because its persona prompts adjust style without shifting truthfulness. The phenomenon is asymmetric rather than a tradeoff: strong alignment makes customization safety-neutral; weak alignment makes customization safety-shifting. The deployment question is therefore not "align or customize" but "how much of the model's safety margin does a given persona consume."

2. Related Work
LLM personality and customization. Serapio-Garc´ia et al. (2025) established psychometric measurement of Big

Five traits in LLMs and showed that traits can be induced and shaped via prompting. Pei et al. (2025) fingerprinted 18 LLMs and found that personality patterns derive from alignment strategy rather than parameter count, a result we lean on when separating alignment effects from scale effects in our two-model design. Duan et al. (2025) showed Big Five traits affect reasoning and creativity in multi-agent settings, and Handa et al. (2025) compared induction methods (incontext learning, PEFT, activation steering) on small models. None of this prior work tests how persona customization interacts with sycophancy or other safety-relevant behaviors as a function of alignment strength.
RLHF, Constitutional AI, and sycophancy. RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) are the dominant alignment training approaches; both inadvertently increase sycophancy as a side effect of optimizing for human-preferred responses (Perez et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024). Wei et al. (2023) showed synthetic-data interventions can reduce sycophancy at training time, and Ranaldi & Pucci (2023) documented sycophantic behavior across instruction-tuned models. This prior work measures sycophancy as a fixed property of a model. We measure it as a persona-conditional property and quantify its variance under persona perturbation, shifting the question from "how sycophantic is this model" to "how stable is sycophancy across user-facing customizations."
Pluralistic alignment. Sorensen et al. (2024) laid out the pluralistic-alignment agenda of accommodating diverse human values rather than collapsing to a single norm, and Kirk et al. (2024) provided participatory data showing how feedback varies across populations. Anwar et al. (2024) catalog open challenges in alignment assurance, including value drift under instruction. Our contribution sits at the intersection: persona prompts are a lightweight pluralism mechanism, and we quantify when they are safety-neutral.
Pre- vs. post-deployment safety adaptation. Concurrent work on lifelong safety adaptation (Kim et al., 2026) addresses the complementary problem of adapting guardrails after deployment via conservative policy induction. floor provides the corresponding pre-deployment audit: a measurement that tells a practitioner whether persona customization is safety-neutral on the candidate model, or whether downstream adaptation mechanisms will be needed in the first place.
