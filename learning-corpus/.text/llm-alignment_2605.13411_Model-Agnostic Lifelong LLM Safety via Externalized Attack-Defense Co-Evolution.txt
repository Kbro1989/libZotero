Model-Agnostic Lifelong LLM Safety via
Externalized Attack-Defense Co-Evolution
Xiaozhe Zhang1, Chaozhuo Li2, Hui Liu1, Shaocheng Yan3, Bingyu Yan4, Qiwei Ye5, Haoliang Li1 1City University of Hong Kong 2Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications 3Wuhan University 4Beihang University 5Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence
Abstract Large language models remain vulnerable to adversarial prompts that elicit harmful outputs. Existing safety paradigms typically couple red-teaming and post-training in a closed, policy-centric loop, causing attack discovery to suffer from rapid saturation and limiting the exposure of novel failure modes, while leaving defenses inefficient, rigid, and difficult to transfer across victim models. To this end, we propose EvoSafety, an LLM safety framework built around persistent, inspectable, and reusable external structures. For red teaming, EvoSafety equips the attack policy with an adversarial skill library, enabling continued vulnerability probing through simple library expansion after saturation, while supporting the evolution of adversarial vectors. For defense learning, EvoSafety replaces model-specific safety fine-tuning with a lightweight auxiliary defense model augmented with memory retrieval. This enables efficient, transferable, and model-agnostic safety improvements, while allowing robustness to be enhanced solely through memory updates. With a single training procedure, the defense policy can operate in both Steer and Guard modes: the former activates the victim model's intrinsic defense mechanisms, while the latter directly filters harmful inputs. Extensive experiments demonstrate the superiority of EvoSafety: in Guard mode, it achieves a 99.61% defense success rate, outperforming Qwen3Guard-8B by 14.13% with only 37.5% of its parameters, while preserving reasoning performance on benign queries. 1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) [Anthropic, 2023, Bai et al., 2023, Dubey et al., 2024, OpenAI, 2023, Team et al., 2023] have emerged as a foundational substrate of modern AI, catalyzing advances across diverse domains such as scientific discovery [Boiko et al., 2023], software engineering [Dong et al., 2025], and beyond [Bubeck et al., 2023]. However, this broad capability is inherently dual-use: adversaries can exploit LLMs through jailbreaking or malicious prompting to elicit harmful outputs, posing serious safety risks [Fang et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2024a, Tang et al., 2024]. Improving the safety of LLMs is therefore a prerequisite for their trustworthy and responsible deployment.
A common recipe for enhancing the safety of LLMs couples red-teaming with safety-oriented posttraining [Bai et al., 2022b, Ge et al., 2024]. Red-teaming simulates an attacker that seeks to expose model vulnerabilities by constructing adversarial prompts that elicit harmful or policy-violating behaviors [Ganguli et al., 2022, Perez et al., 2022]. To improve coverage and scalability, recent work has moved beyond manual prompt engineering [Ahmad et al., 2025, Dominique et al., 2024] toward automated pipelines [Guo et al., 2025a, Yuan et al., 2026, Zhou et al., 2025], among which reinforcement learning (RL)-based methods have emerged as a leading paradigm, as they can adaptively discover and refine novel attack vectors [Guo et al., 2025b, Wen et al., 2025]. Despite this progress, learning for both attack and defense remains largely monolithic, with offensive and defensive knowledge internalized within the parameters of the policy, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). Consequently, safety improvement becomes tightly coupled to a specific victim model and training snapshot [Bai et al., 2022b, Ji et al., 2025], forcing re-optimization whenever the victim changes. Such limitations are particularly severe for large-scale frontier models, for which fine-tuning is substantially more resource-intensive. On the offensive side, policies optimized against a surrogate victim model often exploit model-specific vulnerabilities, producing highly tailored adversarial prompts that are effective against the surrogate but fail to generalize to unseen victim models [Lin et al., 2025]. Moreover, once optimization terminates, their offensive capability tends to plateau, reflecting a limited capacity to continually discover and evolve novel attack vectors [Paulus et al., 2024], which in turn constrains sustained safety improvement. These limitations raise a central question:
How can we enable lifelong, model-agnostic safety improvement? Inspired by the view that human civilization progresses through the continual externalization of cognitive burdens [Carroll, 1991, Sumers et al., 2023], we argue that granting both sides of the safety game access to external knowledge is crucial for advancing LLM safety. From the offensive perspective, if attack-relevant knowledge is externalized and made accessible to the attack policy during training, such a design offers two key benefits. First, a more capable attacker can uncover long-tail vulnerabilities that would otherwise remain hidden. Second, the policy can continue to incorporate distilled knowledge after training, enabling sustained exploration beyond the saturation point, even in a zero-shot setting. Each of these can drive stronger safety improvements [Zheng et al., 2024b]. From the defensive side, if safety knowledge is no longer parameter-bound but is instead instantiated as prompt-level defense mechanisms, defensive capabilities can transfer across model updates without repeated safety retraining and remain extensible to emerging adversarial prompts. To this end, we introduce EvoSafety, a framework that augments both sides of the safety game with persistent, inspectable, and reusable external structures, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b).
